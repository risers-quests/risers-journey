Part 1 · Field Notes

Everything a Seed Needs to Know Before It Travels

You can't write an honest travel diary without knowing the real trip first. This part covers everything: what pollination is, which visitors like which flowers, how a seed forms, and every real way a seed can travel.

Why a flower isn't just decoration

A flower's job was never to look pretty for us. Every color, shape, and smell it has does one job: it moves pollen from one flower to another, usually with a visitor's help. Before your seed's story can even begin, you need to know how that pollen moves, and why a flower goes to all that trouble.

1What pollination actually is

A flower's male part is called the stamen. It has a stalk (the filament) topped with an anther, and the anther makes pollen. A flower's female part is called the pistil. It has a sticky top called the stigma, sitting on a tube called the style. The style leads down to the ovary, where the ovules wait. Pollination is simple: it's just pollen moving from an anther to a stigma — the very first step of my whole story.

Self-pollination is pollen moving within the same flower or the same plant. Cross-pollination is pollen moving between two different plants of the same kind. Most flowers are built for cross-pollination — it makes stronger, more varied seeds. Every bright petal and every strong smell has one job: get a visitor to carry pollen somewhere else.

2Who visits which flower, and why

Every regular visitor is pulled in by clues the flower leaves on purpose:

🐝 Bees

Bees like blue, purple, and yellow flowers. Bees can see UV patterns we can't see — the patterns work like an arrow pointing straight to the nectar. The flower's petals make a flat "landing pad" for the bee.

🦋 Butterflies

Butterflies like red, orange, and pink flowers. They need a place to land. They reach deep nectar with a long tube-shaped mouthpart called a proboscis.

🐦 Hummingbirds & sunbirds

Hummingbirds and sunbirds like red, tube-shaped flowers with lots of nectar deep inside. They don't need a landing pad — they hover in the air. They can't smell well, so scent doesn't matter to them.

🦇 Moths & bats

Moths and bats visit at night. These flowers stay pale or white, so they're easy to see in the dark. They also make a strong sweet smell after dark, since color alone is hard to notice at night.

A few flowers use a stranger trick. Some flies and beetles like the smell of rotting meat, so these flowers copy that smell — they turn dull brown or purple and make a truly bad smell — because that's exactly what those insects want.

Wind pollination is a totally different plan. Grasses, wheat, corn, and most trees don't try to attract any visitor at all. Their flowers are small, dull, and have no scent or nectar — there's no visitor to reward, so why bother? Instead, they release huge amounts of very light pollen into the air, and grow large, feathery stigmas to catch whatever pollen drifts by. Water pollination is rare. It happens mostly in water plants, whose pollen just floats or drifts through the water.

3From flower to seed — this is where I begin

Once pollen lands on a stigma, it grows a tiny tube. The tube grows down through the style until it reaches an ovule inside the ovary — this is called fertilization. Every fertilized ovule becomes a seed. A seed holds a tiny baby plant, plus some food to help it start growing. The ovary around it swells up and becomes a fruit. In science, "fruit" means more than you might think — even things you'd never call "fruit" in a kitchen count: a pea pod, a maple's spinning "key," and a dandelion's fluffy puff are all really fruits.

4Why I can't just stay put

If I dropped straight down and grew right under my own parent, I'd have a problem. I'd be fighting my own parent — and every seed beside me — for the same small amount of sunlight, water, and soil food. I'd also land in dirt that already has whatever pests or sickness my parent attracts. Traveling gives me a real chance to grow without that competition. It also lets my whole species spread into new territory it could never reach otherwise.

5Five ways I could make my journey

💨 Wind

A dandelion's fluffy white pappus catches the wind. A maple seed's "wing" spins like a helicopter blade — this slows its fall so the wind can carry it further. Orchid seeds are almost as light as dust.

🌊 Water

A coconut has a thick, waterproof husk. This lets it float across an ocean for months and still grow on a new shore. Mangrove seeds float upright, and they start growing roots the moment they touch mud.

🐾 Hitchhiking

Burdock and cocklebur seeds are covered in tiny hooks. The hooks catch on fur or clothing, and the seed falls off far from its parent plant. This exact trick is what inspired Velcro.

🍒 Eaten & carried

Berries and cherries are bright and sweet on purpose, so an animal will eat them. The seed inside survives the animal's stomach and comes out somewhere else, with a bit of natural fertilizer.

A fifth way needs no outside help at all: explosive dispersal. A ripe pea pod, or a touch-me-not seed pod, dries out. As it dries, tension builds up inside it — until it suddenly splits open and flings its seeds outward. A squirting cucumber can launch its seeds several metres this way.

History: real seeds that changed what we know

1793
Christian Konrad Sprengel shows, for the first time, that flowers are shaped on purpose to attract certain pollinators. This was a brand new idea back then.
1862
Charles Darwin studies a Madagascar orchid with a very long nectar tube. He predicts a moth with an equally long tongue must exist to pollinate it — even though no one had ever seen one.
1903
Darwin's predicted moth, Xanthopan morganii praedicta, is finally described. But no one photographs it actually pollinating that orchid until 1992 — proving Darwin right, 130 years later.
1941
Swiss engineer George de Mestral looks at burdock burrs stuck to his dog's fur under a microscope. He studies the tiny hooks and invents Velcro — a seed's own trick, copied.

Real-world use: someone is tracking every seed's journey

Ocean scientists have tracked real coconuts drifting for months across whole ocean currents. The coconuts can still sprout after washing up on a totally different island. Some pinecones, like the lodgepole pine, are sealed shut with sticky resin. They only pop open to release seeds after a wildfire's heat melts that resin — a travel plan timed to disaster. And invasive weeds usually spread fastest through a new country by using one of the same five journeys you're choosing between this week.

      📖 Glossary

      Pollination
      Pollen moving from an anther to a stigma.
      Cross-pollination
      Pollen moving between two different plants of the same species.
      Fertilization
      Pollen reaching and joining with an ovule inside the ovary.
      Seed dispersal
      A seed traveling away from its parent plant so it doesn't have to compete with it.
      Pappus
      The fluffy, parachute-like structure on a wind-dispersed seed like a dandelion's.
      Hitchhiking seed
      A seed covered in hooks or barbs that catch on animal fur.
