🌩️ Atmospheric Circulation Forecaster

The Atmosphere Has a Circulatory System Too

Back in Week 1, a pump (your heart) pushed blood through vessels because of pressure differences it created on purpose. The atmosphere runs on the exact same idea, just at planetary scale: uneven heating from the sun creates pressure differences, and air circulates from high pressure to low pressure to even things back out. That circulation is what fronts, storms, hurricanes, and tornadoes actually are, not random chaos, but a real, readable system.

Day 1 · Forecaster Briefing

Fronts, Storms, and the Scales That Rate Them

A forecaster doesn't guess. Every storm on a weather map is the result of real air masses colliding along real boundaries, driven by real pressure differences and steered by a real current of air miles above your head. This briefing covers the whole system before you build the instrument that helps you read it.

1Air masses and the four fronts

An air mass is a huge body of air. It can be thousands of kilometers across. It settles over one region long enough to take on that region's temperature and humidity. Air that sits for days over the warm Gulf of Mexico becomes warm and humid. Air that sits over northern Canada in winter becomes cold and dry. Once an air mass starts moving, it carries that temperature and humidity with it. That is exactly why a "cold snap" or a "muggy spell" can be traced back to one specific air mass sliding into your region.

Weather gets interesting where two different air masses meet. That boundary is called a front. There are four real types of fronts. Each one behaves differently, based on exactly which air mass is doing the moving.

A cold front forms where a cold, dense air mass actively advances into a warmer one. Cold air is denser, so it wedges underneath the warm air like a plow. That forces the warm air up fast. The fast lift builds tall, sometimes violent storm clouds in a narrow band. Cold fronts often bring a sharp line of strong thunderstorms, then a quick temperature drop right behind it. A warm front is the opposite. A warm air mass advances into a retreating cold one. Warm air is less dense, so it can't wedge underneath. Instead, it gently slides up and over the cooler air, like a ramp. That gradual lift spreads clouds and steady, lighter rain over a much wider area, well ahead of where the front actually sits on the ground.

An occluded front happens when a faster-moving cold front catches up to a slower warm front ahead of it. The cold air behind eventually meets the cooler air that was already ahead of the warm front. The warm air mass gets lifted entirely off the ground, squeezed between two masses of cooler air. It's the most complex of the four fronts, and it often brings a mix of rain types as it passes. A stationary front is what happens when neither air mass is winning. The boundary between them just stalls in place for a day or more. Because it doesn't move on, the same stretch of ground can sit under its cloud and rain for a long time. That is why stationary fronts are a common cause of prolonged flooding.

2How storms actually form: pressure and the jet stream

Every storm on a forecast map is built on the same basic engine: a low-pressure system. Air always tries to flow from high pressure toward low pressure, the same way water flows downhill. Where pressure is low, air rises. As it rises, it cools. The water vapor inside it condenses into clouds, often into rain or storm clouds. More air spirals inward along the surface to replace the air that just rose. In the Northern Hemisphere, Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect) bends that inward-spiraling air. That is why it circles counterclockwise around the low. A high-pressure system works in reverse: air sinks, warms, and spreads outward. That is why high pressure almost always means clear, calm skies.

None of this sits still, though. Several miles up, a fast-moving river of air called the jet stream circles the planet. It forms where a large temperature difference exists between the air masses below it. The bigger the temperature contrast, the faster the jet stream moves, sometimes over 200 mph. The jet stream doesn't just sit up there. It actively steers surface storms along its path, roughly the way a current in a river carries a leaf. When the jet stream dips south in a big wavy loop, it can drag cold air and storms far out of their usual track. Sometimes it gets "stuck" in one of those loops for days or weeks at a time. The storms riding it can stall too. That is part of why some regions get locked into an extended drought while a region right next door floods, at the same time, for the same underlying reason.

3Severe weather: hurricanes, tornadoes, and their real scales

A hurricane starts as ordinary thunderstorms over tropical ocean water. But it only forms once that water is warm enough, at least about 26.5°C (80°F), all the way down to a real depth, not just at the surface. Warm water evaporates fast. As that moist air rises, it releases heat as it condenses. That heat is called latent heat. It warms the air around it further and makes the air rise even faster. This is a feedback loop that keeps feeding the storm more energy. The Coriolis effect (the same rotation-bending force from the last section) gives the rising air a spin. If conditions stay right, the thunderstorms organize around a shared center of low pressure. That builds the tight, spinning system with an eye and eyewall that we call a hurricane. Once a hurricane moves over land or over cooler water, its fuel source, warm ocean moisture, is cut off. It weakens fast.

Meteorologists rate a hurricane's strength using the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale. It is based purely on sustained wind speed. Category 1 starts at 74 mph. Category 2 starts at 96 mph. Category 3, the start of a "major hurricane", starts at 111 mph. Category 4 starts at 130 mph. Category 5 starts at 157 mph and above.

A tornado forms through a very different process. It happens almost always inside a supercell, a single, long-lived thunderstorm with a rotating updraft called a mesocyclone. That rotation usually starts sideways. Wind shear is wind blowing at different speeds or in different directions at different heights. Wind shear can set a whole tube of air spinning horizontally, low to the ground. The supercell's strong updraft can catch that spinning tube and tilt it vertical. The same effect that stretches a figure skater's spin faster as they pull their arms in also stretches and tightens this column of air. That speeds up its spin dramatically as it narrows into a visible funnel that can reach the ground.

A tornado usually destroys any instrument in its own path. That means meteorologists can't just measure its wind speed directly in most cases. Instead, the Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale rates a tornado from EF0 to EF5. It works by surveying the actual damage left behind afterward. Meteorologists use known engineering data about how much wind it takes to cause that specific kind of damage. Then they work backward to an estimated wind speed.

🌀 Hurricane

Needs warm ocean water (≥26.5°C) and Coriolis spin, rated by sustained wind speed.

🌪️ Tornado

Forms inside a supercell, when wind shear's horizontal spin gets tilted and stretched vertical.

📊 Two different scales

Saffir-Simpson measures hurricane wind directly; the EF scale estimates tornado wind from damage.

4Put together: how forecasters actually predict weather

No single tool tells a forecaster everything. Real forecasting combines several real tools into one system. Satellites orbit far overhead and capture visible and infrared images of cloud systems. That lets forecasters track a hurricane's entire structure, or watch a front sweep across a continent, even over open ocean, where there are no ground stations at all. Doppler radar does something a plain camera can't. It doesn't just show where precipitation is. It measures how fast that precipitation is moving toward or away from the radar. That is exactly what lets forecasters spot the tight rotation inside a supercell, sometimes called a hook echo on radar, and issue a tornado warning before a funnel cloud is ever actually seen. Weather balloons, called radiosondes, launch twice a day from hundreds of stations worldwide. They carry instruments straight up through the atmosphere, measuring real temperature, humidity, and wind at every altitude on the way up. Computer weather models take all of that real, current data. They run it through the same physics equations that govern air, pressure, and moisture, on supercomputers. That projects forward what the atmosphere will do next, hours, days, or over a week out.

Put together, these four tools do more than describe the weather happening right now. They let a forecaster warn people what's coming before it arrives.

History: how forecasting became a real science

1854
Robert FitzRoy sets up the UK's Meteorological Department and begins issuing the first public daily weather predictions, he's the one who coined the word "forecast" for them.
1920s
Norwegian scientists at the Bergen School, led by Vilhelm Bjerknes and his son Jacob Bjerknes, develop the polar front theory, the first real model explaining storms as air masses colliding along fronts. It's still the basic framework behind Section 1 of this briefing.
1960
TIROS-1, the first weather satellite, launches, for the first time, forecasters can actually see a storm's real shape and motion from space instead of piecing it together from scattered ground reports.
1971
Ted Fujita introduces the original Fujita Scale for rating tornado damage (refined into the Enhanced Fujita Scale in 2007); the same era sees Herbert Saffir and Robert Simpson develop the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale still used today.

Real-world application: why advanced forecasting actually matters

A hurricane's forecast track, days out, gives coastal cities enough lead time to actually run an evacuation before roads jam up. Get that timing wrong in either direction, and people either evacuate needlessly or don't leave in time. A tornado warning from Doppler radar's rotation signature can give a town minutes of real warning before a funnel cloud is ever spotted by eye. That is often the entire difference between reaching shelter and not. Farmers use seasonal and short-term forecasts to time planting, irrigation, and harvest around real frost and rain risk, instead of guesswork. Airlines route entire flights around jet-stream position and storm cells, to avoid dangerous turbulence and save fuel. None of this works without the whole system in this briefing: a front you can name, a pressure system you understand, and a real number on a real scale, not just a guess that "the weather looks bad."

    Day 3 · Build Day

    Make a Real Cloud, Right in a Jar

    A cloud is just warm, moist air meeting cold air fast. You can make that happen for real, in about 15 minutes, using nothing but a jar, hot water, and ice.

    What you're building

    A real, physical cloud, not a picture of one. Hot water in the bottom of the jar fills the air above it with water vapor, warm and moist, exactly like the air near the ground on a hot, humid day. Placing a cold lid of ice right on top of the jar suddenly cools the top layer of that moist air. Cooled fast enough, the water vapor condenses back into tiny liquid droplets, millions of them, floating together. Those tiny floating droplets are, literally, a cloud, the same process that forms a real cloud in the sky, just much faster and much smaller.

    Finished, it should look like: a swirl of visible white fog or cloud appearing inside your jar within seconds of the cold lid going on, thickest right where the warm, moist air meets the cold surface.

    Why this build matters

    Real clouds form the exact same way, warm, moist air rises and cools, and its water vapor condenses into visible droplets. Where that warm, moist air meets a cold air mass fast and hard is exactly where storms tend to form, the same fronts you read about on Day 1. Your jar is a small, fast version of the same process happening for real, at a much bigger scale, along a real weather front.

      📖 Glossary

      Air mass
      A huge body of air that has taken on one region's temperature and humidity, then moves while carrying those properties with it.
      Cold front
      A boundary where advancing cold, dense air wedges under warmer air, lifting it fast and building sharp, sometimes strong storms.
      Warm front
      A boundary where advancing warm air slides gently up and over retreating cooler air, bringing wide, steady rain well ahead of it.
      Occluded front
      A boundary formed when a faster cold front catches a slower warm front, lifting the warm air mass entirely off the ground.
      Stationary front
      A boundary between two air masses where neither is advancing, so it can bring rain to the same place for days.
      Jet stream
      A fast-moving river of air high in the atmosphere that steers surface storms along its path.
      Coriolis effect
      The bending force from Earth's rotation that curves the path of moving air, giving storms their spin.
      Supercell
      A single, long-lived, rotating thunderstorm capable of producing the most powerful tornadoes.
      Wind shear
      A change in wind speed or direction at different heights in the atmosphere, which can start a tornado's rotation.
      Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale
      A 1–5 scale rating a hurricane's strength by its sustained wind speed, from 74 mph (Category 1) up.
      Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale
      A 0–5 scale rating a tornado's strength by estimating wind speed from surveyed damage, since instruments rarely survive the tornado itself.
      Doppler radar
      Radar that measures how fast precipitation is moving toward or away from it, letting forecasters spot rotation inside a storm.
