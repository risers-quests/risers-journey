Nothing Created, Nothing Destroyed

Every chemical reaction rearranges atoms into new molecules, it never creates a single new atom and never destroys one. A balanced equation is just that rule, written down and checked. Get the count wrong on either side, and the equation is lying about what actually happened.

Day 1 · Systems Briefing

What a Chemical Equation Actually Promises

A chemical equation is a promise about atoms: every single one on the left has to show up again on the right. This briefing covers what that promise means and how to check it, before you calculate anything with it yourself.

1A chemical equation is a before-and-after count

A chemical equation shows a reaction happening: the reactants (what you start with) on the left, an arrow, then the products (what you end up with) on the right. Hydrogen and oxygen reacting to form water is written H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Read left to right, hydrogen gas plus oxygen gas produces water.

Here's the part that's easy to miss: a reaction doesn't create brand-new atoms, and it doesn't destroy any either. It only rearranges the atoms that were already there into new molecules. This is called the law of conservation of mass, every atom present in the reactants has to still be present, somewhere, in the products. If an equation shows 4 hydrogen atoms on the left and only 2 on the right, that equation is claiming 2 hydrogen atoms simply vanished, and that never actually happens.

2Coefficients, subscripts, and why equations need balancing

A chemical formula uses two different numbers, and mixing them up is the single most common mistake. A subscript is the small number written after an element inside a formula, in H₂O, the subscript 2 means each water molecule has 2 hydrogen atoms. A coefficient is a number written in front of an entire formula, in 2H₂O, that coefficient 2 means there are 2 whole water molecules, each one still built the same way, still 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom each.

That difference matters enormously when you're counting atoms. 2H₂O means 2 separate water molecules, so the coefficient multiplies everything inside that formula: 2 × 2 = 4 hydrogen atoms total, and 2 × 1 = 2 oxygen atoms total. Only coefficients can be changed to balance an equation, subscripts are fixed, since changing a subscript would turn the molecule into a completely different substance.

3Real strategies for balancing an equation

Balancing an equation means adding coefficients until every element has the exact same total count on both sides. A reliable way to do it: count every atom of every element on both sides first, find the element that's most unequal, add a coefficient to fix it, then recount everything, since fixing one element can throw another one off. Keep repeating until every single element matches.

Take H₂ + O₂ → H₂O. Counting atoms: the left has 2 hydrogen and 2 oxygen, the right has 2 hydrogen and only 1 oxygen. Oxygen is unequal, so put a coefficient of 2 in front of the water: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Recount: now the right side has 2 × 2 = 4 hydrogen and 2 × 1 = 2 oxygen, oxygen matches now, but hydrogen doesn't, the left still only has 2. Fix that by putting a coefficient of 2 in front of the hydrogen gas too: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Recount one more time: left has 2 × 2 = 4 hydrogen and 2 oxygen, right has 4 hydrogen and 2 oxygen. Every element matches. The equation is balanced.

🔧 Quick Check

In the balancing strategy above, why wasn't the equation finished as soon as oxygen matched on both sides?

🔧 Quick Check

The fully balanced equation is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Count the total hydrogen atoms on the left side, and the total hydrogen atoms on the right side, and confirm they match.

4Put together, why this matters beyond the page

A real chemist checking an equation isn't just following a classroom rule, they're confirming the equation actually describes something physically possible. An unbalanced equation is claiming atoms appeared from nowhere or disappeared into nothing, and matter simply doesn't behave that way. Chemical engineers use balanced equations to calculate exactly how much raw material a factory needs to produce a set amount of product, without balancing correctly first, those numbers would be wrong before any real chemistry even started.

      📖 Glossary

      Reactant
      A starting substance in a chemical reaction, written on the left side of the equation's arrow.
      Product
      A substance produced by a chemical reaction, written on the right side of the equation's arrow.
      Law of conservation of mass
      Atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction, only rearranged into new molecules.
      Coefficient
      A number written in front of an entire chemical formula, showing how many whole molecules there are.
      Subscript
      A small number written after an element inside a formula, fixed, showing how many atoms of that element are in one molecule.
      Balanced equation
      A chemical equation where every element has the exact same total atom count on both sides.
      Molecule
      A group of atoms bonded together, the smallest unit of a chemical compound.
      Chemical formula
      A written shorthand for a molecule, showing which elements it's made of and how many atoms of each.
