Day 1 · Full Body Scan

What Every Atom Is Actually Built From

You can't map a body without knowing what every organ does. This briefing covers the basics: the particles every atom has, the one number that tells you which element it is, and why the particles sit where they do.

Why matter is never really solid

Everything around you is made of atoms, this desk, the air, even your own hand. Atoms are the basic building blocks of all matter. An atom is not a solid little ball. It's a tiny structure with different parts in different places, doing different jobs, just like your own body has different organs doing different jobs. Before you map your own atom-body, you need to know what's inside every single atom.

1The three particles every atom is built from

Every atom is built from the same three tiny particles. The proton carries a positive charge. It sits packed inside the nucleus, the atom's center, think of it as your heartbeat, the part that drives everything else. The neutron carries no charge at all. It sits right next to the protons, also in the nucleus, like your skeleton, holding everything together without adding a charge of its own. The electron carries a negative charge. It moves around outside the nucleus, in a space called the electron cloud, like your circulation, always moving through the outer layers of the body. The nucleus is tiny. If an atom were the size of your whole body, the nucleus would be about the size of one grain of rice at your core. But that one small grain holds almost all of the atom's weight, a proton or neutron weighs about 1,800 times as much as one electron. Almost all of an atom's space is the empty area in the electron cloud around that one packed core.

2What makes one element different from every other element

Every element has one number that is only its own, no other element shares it. That number is the atomic number: the exact number of protons in an atom's nucleus. Change the proton count, and you've changed the element completely. No two elements ever share the same atomic number, it's like an ID that's never given to anyone else. An element is a pure substance made of only one type of atom. Every single atom of that element has that one exact atomic number, every time.

Here are three real examples. Hydrogen has exactly 1 proton, atomic number 1, the simplest atom there is. Carbon has exactly 6 protons, atomic number 6. Oxygen has exactly 8 protons, atomic number 8. Count the protons, and you know exactly which element you're looking at. One more fact: a neutral atom (no leftover charge) always has the same number of electrons as protons. That's why the positive and negative charges cancel out to zero.

🔢 Atomic number

The exact number of protons in the nucleus. No two elements ever share one, it's the element's ID.

🧪 Element

A pure substance made of only one type of atom. Every atom of it has the same exact atomic number.

⚖️ Neutral atom

Electron count equals proton count. The positive and negative charges cancel out to zero.

3Why particles don't just sit anywhere they want

Nothing in an atom sits by accident, every particle has a real reason for where it is. Electrons don't just drift around in the electron cloud. They sit in shells (also called energy levels), set at fixed distances from the nucleus, like blood circulating through set loops instead of just anywhere. The shell closest to the nucleus fills first, and it can only hold 2 electrons. Once that shell is full, the next electrons go into the second shell, which can hold up to 8. Electrons closer to the nucleus are held more tightly. That's why an atom always fills its inner shell completely before any electron moves to the next one out. Take Carbon: 6 electrons total. The first 2 fill the first shell, and the other 4 sit in the second shell. Oxygen has 8 electrons: 2 fill the first shell, and 6 sit in the second shell.

The nucleus needs neutrons for its own reason. Every proton carries the same positive charge, and same charges push each other away. So a nucleus packed with several protons would fly apart from that push alone. Neutrons sit between the protons and hold the nucleus together, like a skeleton holding a crowded core together instead of letting it push itself apart. That's why every element past hydrogen needs neutrons in its nucleus, not just protons.

Reading the periodic table, the full chart on every element

The periodic table is a chart of every known element, all in one place. Every element gets its own spot, in order of atomic number, left to right, top to bottom, starting with hydrogen at atomic number 1. Each row is called a period. A new period starts every time a new outer electron shell begins. Each column is called a group (or family). Elements in the same column tend to act alike, because they have a similar number of electrons in their outer shell.

History: how we found out bodies this small even existed

1803
John Dalton says every element is made of matching atoms, and different elements are made of different atoms. It was the first real, testable idea about atoms.
1897
J.J. Thomson discovers the electron, the first tiny particle ever found inside an atom. This proved atoms could be split into smaller parts. He pictured the atom like a "plum pudding": a positive blob with negative electrons stuck inside it.
1911
Ernest Rutherford runs an experiment and finds that an atom's positive charge and almost all its mass are packed into one tiny, dense spot, the nucleus. This proved the plum-pudding idea wrong.
1913
Niels Bohr says electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed shells at set distances, like planets orbiting the Sun. This is the model our whole reading is built on.

Real-world application: doctors read your own hydrogen atoms

An MRI scanner doesn't cut you open to see inside your body. It detects the protons inside the hydrogen atoms that make up most of the water in your body. A human body is roughly 60% water. So doctors can build a full picture of what's happening inside you just by reading the signal those hydrogen protons give off inside a powerful magnet. The exact same particle this whole reading is built on is what makes that scan possible.

      A pure substance made of only one kind of atom.

      A chart that organizes every known element by atomic number.
