What Are Things Made Of?

No two materials feel the same in your hands. Today you'll learn how to test and sort materials for yourself, and why every object is made from the material that fits its job best.

Prepared for: Gabby

Day 1 · Read and Learn

What Materials Are, and How We Test Them

Let's learn the real facts: what a material is, how to test one, five materials you see every day, why the material has to match the job, and why reusing and recycling matter.

Everything around you right now — your chair, your shoe, your cup — is made of something. Today you'll learn what that "something" is called, and how to test it for yourself, using nothing but your own hands and eyes.

1What is a material?

A material is what an object is made of. Wood, metal, plastic, glass, and fabric are all materials. Some objects are made of just one material. Other objects, like a pencil, are made of several.

We don't have to guess what a material is like. We can test it with our own hands and eyes, using a few simple checks.

Is it hard or soft? Press it gently. A rock is hard. A pillow is soft.

Is it bendy or stiff? Try to bend it a little. A rubber band is bendy. A ruler is stiff.

🔍 Four ways to test a material
Simple checks you can do with just your hands and eyes.
1Hard or soft? — press it gently
2Bendy or stiff? — bend it a little
3See-through or not? — hold it to the light
4Heavy or light? — feel its weight in your hand

Is it see-through or solid? Hold it up to a light or a window. A clean window is see-through. A wall is solid.

Is it heavy or light? Feel its weight in your hand. A brick is heavy. A feather is light.

2Five materials you see every day

Now that you know how to test a material, let's meet five you already see every single day: wood, metal, plastic, glass, and fabric.

Wood comes from trees. It is a little bit bendy, but strong. You can find wood in tables, doors, and pencils.

Plastic is made by people, usually starting from oil. It can be hard or soft, and it can be almost any color. You can find plastic in toys, bottles, and toothbrushes.

Fabric is made of tiny threads, woven or knitted together. It is soft and bendy. You can find fabric in shirts, blankets, and backpacks.

🪵 Wood

Comes from trees. A little bendy, but strong. Used for furniture, doors, and pencils.

🔧 Metal

Dug from the ground as rock called ore. Hard, heavy, and strong. Used for spoons, nails, and cars.

🪟 Glass

Made by heating sand until it melts, then cooling it. Hard, but see-through. Used for windows, cups, and jars.

Metal is dug out of the ground as a rock called ore. It is hard, heavy, and strong. You can find metal in spoons, nails, and cars.

Glass is made by heating sand until it melts, then letting it cool and harden. It is hard, but you can see right through it. You can find glass in windows, cups, and jars.

3The right material for the job

People don't pick a material by accident. They pick it because of what it can actually do — the material has to match the job.

Windows are made of glass. Glass is see-through, so light can pass through it and you can see outside. Wood or metal would block the light completely.

Chairs are usually made of wood or metal. Both are strong and stiff, so they can hold you up without bending or breaking.

Shirts are made of fabric. Fabric is soft and bendy, so it can move with your body and feel comfortable on your skin.

🪟🪑👕 Same job, different material
The material is picked because of what the job actually needs.
Window is glass — see-through, so light gets in.
Chair is wood or metal — stiff, so it holds you up.
Shirt is fabric — soft and bendy, so it's comfortable.

If you picked the wrong material, the job just wouldn't work. A glass shirt would be stiff and could shatter. A fabric window wouldn't let you see outside at all.

4Reusing and recycling materials

Materials don't have to be used just once. Many of them can be used again, or turned into something brand new.

Reusing means using an object again, for the same job or a new one — like using an old jar to hold pencils, or using a paper bag for lunch again.

Recycling means breaking a material down and turning it into a new object. Glass, metal, paper, and many plastics can all be recycled.

A recycled metal can might become part of a brand new can, or even part of a bicycle. A recycled glass jar can be melted down and made into a new jar or bottle.

Reusing and recycling both help. They mean fewer new materials have to be dug up or made from scratch, and less trash ends up buried in the ground.

🌍 Why It Matters

Why is it a good idea to reuse or recycle materials instead of always throwing them away?

History: how we found out

c. 3300 BCE
People of the Bronze Age learn to mix copper and tin together. The new metal, bronze, is harder and stronger than either metal alone — an early lesson that mixing materials can make something better than the parts you started with.
1907
Leo Baekeland invents Bakelite, one of the very first fully man-made plastics. Before this, most "plastic-like" objects were made from natural materials, like tree sap or animal horn.
1970
The first Earth Day is held in the United States. It helps kick off the modern recycling movement, encouraging people everywhere to reuse and recycle materials instead of always throwing them away.

Real-world use: real people use these same facts

Real engineers and builders use these exact same ideas every single day. When engineers build a bridge, they choose strong metal and stone, not soft fabric. When designers make a raincoat, they choose a material that keeps water out. Recycling centers sort glass, metal, plastic, and paper into separate piles, so each material can be melted down or reused to make something brand new.
