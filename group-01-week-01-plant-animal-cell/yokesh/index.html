Part 1 · Site Survey

Everything a Cell Actually Needs

You can't plan a city without knowing what every building actually does. This briefing covers the full picture: what every cell has in common, what only a plant cell has, and exactly why those differences exist.

Why one tiny structure runs everything alive

Every living thing, from a single bacterium to you, is built out of cells — the basic working unit of life. A cell isn't empty space with some parts floating in it; it's a fully organized, self-running structure, with different sections doing different jobs, the same way a real city has different districts doing different jobs. Before you can plan your own cell-city, you need to know exactly what's already inside every single cell, plant or animal.

1What every cell has, no exceptions

Every plant and animal cell shares five core parts. The cell membrane is the outer boundary, controlling exactly what enters and leaves — a city's checkpoint gate. The cytoplasm is the jelly-like fluid filling the cell, where everything else sits and reactions happen — the city's open ground. The nucleus holds the cell's DNA and controls all its activity — the city's control center. Mitochondria release energy from food through respiration — the city's power plant. Ribosomes build proteins the cell needs — the city's factories.

2What only a plant cell has

A plant cell has three extra buildings an animal cell's city never needs. The cell wall is a rigid outer layer made of cellulose, sitting outside the membrane — the city's outer fortification wall, giving the whole structure a fixed shape it never has to change. The chloroplast contains chlorophyll and makes food from sunlight through photosynthesis — a solar farm generating the city's entire energy supply from scratch, something an animal cell's city has to import instead. A large, permanent vacuole stores water and keeps the cell firm — a water tower keeping the whole city pressurized.

🧱 Cell wall

Rigid, made of cellulose, sits outside the membrane. Gives the cell a fixed shape and real structural support.

☀️ Chloroplast

Contains chlorophyll (what makes plants green). Captures sunlight to make the plant's own food — photosynthesis.

💧 Permanent vacuole

Large, filled with cell sap. Keeps the cell firm and upright by pushing outward against the cell wall.

3Why the differences actually matter

None of this is random. A plant can't get up and move to find food or escape danger — so it needs a fixed, upright structure it can rely on permanently, and it needs to make its own food since it can't go looking for any. That's exactly what the cell wall and chloroplast solve. An animal, on the other hand, needs to move, change shape, and squeeze through gaps — a rigid wall would make that impossible, so animal cells stay flexible, with only a membrane at the edge.

History: how we found out cities this small even existed

1665
Robert Hooke examines a thin slice of cork under a microscope, sees tiny box-like compartments, and names them "cells" — the first time the word is ever used this way.
1670s
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek builds far more powerful microscopes and becomes the first person to observe living cells and microorganisms moving under one.
1838–1839
Matthias Schleiden (studying plants) and Theodor Schwann (studying animals) jointly propose Cell Theory: all living things are made of cells.
1855
Rudolf Virchow adds the missing piece: every new cell comes from an existing cell dividing, not from anywhere else.

Real-world application: a plant's rigid wall shows up everywhere

Cotton fabric is almost pure cellulose pulled straight from plant cell walls, and paper is made by breaking those same walls down into pulp — two entire industries built on one plant cell part. Multiplied across billions of cells, that same rigid wall is the whole reason wood can hold up a tree, a house frame, or a piece of furniture with no skeleton at all.
