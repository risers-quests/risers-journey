Day 1 · Systems Briefing

Fossil Fuels, Hydrocarbons, and Plastics · the Real Chain

Three real materials, one continuous chain, and a real catch underneath all of it. This briefing covers exactly how fossil fuels form and what they actually turn into, before you build something real of your own.

1Hydrocarbons: molecules built from carbon and hydrogen

The word "hydrocarbon" sounds like it could mean almost anything. It doesn't. A hydrocarbon is a molecule made of exactly two elements: carbon and hydrogen. Nothing else. The simplest hydrocarbon is methane (CH₄). It has one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms. Some hydrocarbons are much bigger, a long chain of dozens of carbon atoms. But every hydrocarbon, big or small, is built from only those same two elements.

Hydrocarbons don't float around loose in nature. They are packed inside three fossil fuels: crude oil, natural gas, and coal. Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of different hydrocarbon molecules, some short chains, some long chains. That's exactly why crude oil gets separated, or refined, into different products like gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. Natural gas is mostly methane, the simplest hydrocarbon there is. Coal is a little different. It is still a fossil fuel, and it is still rich in carbon. But it is not a clean hydrocarbon mixture the way oil and gas are, real coal also has other elements mixed in, like sulfur and nitrogen.

2How fossils and fossil fuels actually form

Fossil fuels don't form the way a lot of people picture it. There is no single "dinosaur soup" underground. Oil and natural gas form mostly from ancient marine organisms, tiny plankton and algae. Trillions of them lived, died, and sank to ancient seafloors. Layer after layer of sediment buried that organic material. Over time, it turned into a waxy substance called kerogen. If it gets buried deep enough, thousands of feet down, heat and pressure slowly convert that kerogen into the hydrocarbons that make up oil and natural gas. Those hydrocarbons collect inside porous source rock.

Coal forms a different way. Instead of marine plankton, it starts with ancient swamp and forest plants. Dead plant material piled up faster than it could fully decompose in low-oxygen swamp water. Buried and squeezed over millions of years, that plant material moved through real stages, one after another: peat, then lignite, then bituminous coal, then anthracite. Depth, heat, and pressure all increased at each stage.

None of this happens fast enough for a person to watch. Real source rock and real coal seams take millions of years to form. That's exactly why fossil fuels are called a non-renewable resource. Humans are pumping and mining them out far, far faster than the Earth could ever replace them.

🔬 Burial over geologic time, surface to source rock
Four stages, each one deeper, older, and hotter than the one above it.
1Surface / seafloor, living things die and settle into mud and sand
2Shallow burial, new sediment piles on top; heat and pressure start building
3Deep burial, thousands of feet down; kerogen slowly converts toward oil and gas
4Source rock, oil and gas sit trapped in porous rock, millions of years later

Coal forms through a related but separate path: ancient swamp plant material, not marine plankton, buried and compressed with far less oxygen around it, peat, then lignite, then bituminous coal, then anthracite, as depth and pressure increase.

3What plastics actually are

Plastic isn't dug up or mined the way coal is. It's manufactured. A plastic is a synthetic polymer, a long chain molecule. It's built by chemically bonding together thousands of small repeating units, called monomers. Most plastic starts as crude oil or natural gas. That gets refined into petrochemicals like ethylene and propylene, small hydrocarbon molecules. Those small molecules then get linked together into long polymer chains.

Different starting monomers make different plastics, with different properties. Polyethylene (from ethylene) is lightweight and flexible, good for bags and bottles. Polypropylene (from propylene) is stiffer and handles heat better, good for bottle caps and food containers. PVC (from vinyl chloride) is rigid and weather-resistant, good for pipes and siding. Plastic is used so widely because of one practical combination: it's durable, lightweight, cheap to produce, and it can be molded into almost any shape while it's still soft.

🧵 Polyethylene (PE)

The most common plastic by weight, bags, bottles, and film. Built from ethylene monomers.

🪣 Polypropylene (PP)

Stiffer and more heat-resistant, bottle caps, food containers, rope.

🚰 PVC

Polyvinyl chloride, rigid and weather-resistant, pipes, siding, some packaging.

4Put together, it's a system · with a real catch

Trace the whole chain and it's one continuous system, not three separate topics. A fossil fuel (oil, gas, or coal) is buried, ancient organic material. Inside it are hydrocarbon molecules. Refine those hydrocarbons and chemically bond them together, and you get a plastic. Every plastic item within reach right now traces back through that exact same chain.

That chain also comes with a real catch. The properties that make plastic so useful, cheap, lightweight, and built to last, are the exact same properties that make it a problem once it's thrown away. A plastic bottle resists breaking down for years on a shelf. It also resists breaking down for centuries in a landfill or the ocean. Instead, it breaks into smaller and smaller pieces, called microplastics. Burning fossil fuels for energy also releases carbon dioxide, which adds to climate change. Recycling helps, but it doesn't fully solve the problem. Different plastic types can't all be recycled together. Quality gets worse each time plastic is reprocessed. And a large share of plastic waste still isn't recycled at all. That's exactly why using less plastic in the first place, and developing real alternatives like biodegradable plastics, matters just as much as recycling does.

Real-world application: this chain gets worked every day

Petroleum geologists study rock layers to find where source rock actually sits, before anyone drills a single well. Petrochemical engineers run the crackers and reactors that turn crude oil and natural gas into the ethylene and propylene that become real plastic products. Materials scientists test which resin type a product should be made from, balancing cost, durability, and how easily it can be recycled later. Paleontologists use that same buried-and-preserved idea, on a different material, to read Earth's ancient history from actual fossils. None of this is guesswork. Every one of those jobs starts from the same real chain you're about to trace yourself.

🕵️ Refinery Detective · worked trace

Every plastic product traces backward through the exact same four links, in order: product → polymer → hydrocarbon → raw fossil fuel. Here's that trace worked all the way through for one real product, a plastic shopping bag:

  1. Product: a plastic shopping bag.
  2. Polymer: it's polyethylene (PE), from the info-cards above, the most common plastic by weight, lightweight and flexible, which is exactly what a bag needs to be.
  3. Hydrocarbon: polyethylene is built by chemically bonding together thousands of ethylene (C₂H₄) monomers, a small, 2-carbon hydrocarbon.
  4. Raw fossil fuel: that ethylene is refined out of natural gas or crude oil, as a petrochemical, before it's ever bonded into a polymer at all.

Traced backward, the full chain reads: shopping bag ← polyethylene (polymer) ← ethylene (hydrocarbon) ← natural gas / crude oil (fossil fuel).

History: how we found and used this chain

~300 mya
During the Carboniferous Period, vast swamp forests cover much of the Earth. Dead plant material piles up faster than it can fully decompose in the swamps' low-oxygen water, the start of most of the coal deposits still being mined today.
1859
Edwin Drake drills the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, launching the modern petroleum industry and the search for buried source rock worldwide.
1907
Leo Baekeland invents Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic, made entirely from fossil-fuel-derived chemicals, not adapted from any natural material like earlier plastics had been.

      📖 Glossary

      Hydrocarbon
      A molecule made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms, the building block of fossil fuels and most plastics.
      Fossil fuel
      An energy resource, oil, natural gas, or coal, formed from the buried remains of ancient living things over millions of years.
      Fossil
      The preserved remains or traces of an ancient organism, bones, shells, or footprints, found in rock.
      Kerogen
      The waxy, carbon-rich material inside buried source rock that heat and pressure slowly convert into oil and natural gas.
      Crude oil
      The raw, unrefined liquid mixture of hydrocarbons pumped from the ground, before it's separated into fuels and other products.
      Polymer
      A long chain molecule made of many smaller repeating units, called monomers, bonded together.
      Monomer
      A single small molecule that bonds with many others like it to form a long chain called a polymer.
      Petrochemical
      A chemical made from crude oil or natural gas, used as a raw ingredient for products like plastic.
      Non-renewable resource
      A resource that forms so slowly, over millions of years, that using it up happens far faster than nature can replace it.
      Recycling
      Reprocessing a used material into new material instead of throwing it away, though not every plastic type can be recycled the same way.
