Day 1 · Systems Briefing

Fossil Fuels, Hydrocarbons, and Plastics · the Real Chain

Three real materials, one continuous chain, and a real catch underneath all of it. This briefing covers exactly how fossil fuels form and what they actually turn into, before you build something real of your own.

1Hydrocarbons: molecules built from carbon and hydrogen

The word "hydrocarbon" sounds like it could mean anything. It doesn't. A hydrocarbon is a molecule made of just two elements: carbon and hydrogen. Nothing else is in it. The simplest hydrocarbon is methane (CH₄). It has one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms, joined together. Some hydrocarbons are small, like methane. Some are big, long chains with dozens of carbon atoms. But every hydrocarbon, big or small, is made from only carbon and hydrogen.

Hydrocarbons are not found floating around loose. They are packed inside three fossil fuels: crude oil, natural gas, and coal. Crude oil is a mix of thousands of different hydrocarbon molecules. Some are short. Some are long. That is why crude oil gets separated, refined, into different products, like gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel. Natural gas is mostly methane. Coal is a bit different. It is still a fossil fuel, and it is still rich in carbon. But it is not a clean mix of hydrocarbons the way oil and gas are. Coal also has other things mixed in, like sulfur and nitrogen.

2How fossils and fossil fuels actually form

Fossil fuels do not form the way most people picture it. There is no giant "dinosaur soup" underground. Oil and natural gas form mostly from ancient marine organisms, tiny plankton and algae. Trillions of them lived, died, and sank to the bottom of ancient seas. Layer after layer of sediment buried them. Over a very long time, that buried material turned into a waxy substance called kerogen. If it gets buried deep enough, thousands of feet down, heat and pressure slowly turn that kerogen into the hydrocarbons inside oil and natural gas. Those hydrocarbons collect inside porous source rock.

Coal forms a different way. It does not start with plankton in the ocean. It starts with ancient swamp and forest plants. Dead plants piled up faster than they could rot away in the low-oxygen swamp water. Buried and squeezed over millions of years, that plant material slowly changed, one stage at a time: first peat, then lignite, then bituminous coal, then anthracite. Each stage is deeper, hotter, and under more pressure than the one before it.

None of this happens fast enough for a person to watch. Real source rock and real coal seams take millions of years to form. That is exactly why fossil fuels are called a non-renewable resource. People are pumping and mining them out of the ground far, far faster than the Earth could ever replace them.

🔬 Burial over geologic time, surface to source rock
Four stages, each one deeper, older, and hotter than the one above it.
1Surface / seafloor, living things die and settle into mud and sand
2Shallow burial, new sediment piles on top; heat and pressure start building
3Deep burial, thousands of feet down; kerogen slowly converts toward oil and gas
4Source rock, oil and gas sit trapped in porous rock, millions of years later

Coal forms through a related but separate path: ancient swamp plant material, not marine plankton, buried and compressed with far less oxygen around it, peat, then lignite, then bituminous coal, then anthracite, as depth and pressure increase.

3What plastics actually are

Plastic is not dug up or mined the way coal is. It is manufactured, made in a factory. A plastic is a synthetic polymer. That means it is a long chain molecule. It is built by bonding together thousands of small repeating pieces, called monomers. Most plastic starts out as crude oil or natural gas. That gets refined into petrochemicals, like ethylene and propylene. Those are small hydrocarbon molecules. They get linked together, one after another, into a long polymer chain.

Different starting monomers make different plastics, with different properties. Polyethylene comes from ethylene. It is light and bendy, good for bags and bottles. Polypropylene comes from propylene. It is stiffer, and handles heat better, good for bottle caps and food containers. PVC comes from vinyl chloride. It is stiff and stands up to weather, good for pipes and siding. Plastic is used everywhere for one simple reason: it is tough, light, cheap to make, and it can be shaped into almost anything while it is still soft.

🧵 Polyethylene (PE)

The most common plastic by weight, bags, bottles, and film. Built from ethylene monomers.

🪣 Polypropylene (PP)

Stiffer and more heat-resistant, bottle caps, food containers, rope.

🚰 PVC

Polyvinyl chloride, rigid and weather-resistant, pipes, siding, some packaging.

4Put together, it's a system · with a real catch

Trace the whole chain, and it is one system, not three separate topics. A fossil fuel (oil, gas, or coal) is buried, ancient organic material. Inside it are hydrocarbon molecules. Refine those hydrocarbons and bond them together, and you get a plastic. Every plastic item near you right now traces back through that exact same chain.

That chain also comes with a real catch. The things that make plastic so useful, cheap, light, built to last, are the same things that make it a problem once it's thrown away. A plastic bottle resists breaking down for years on a shelf. It also resists breaking down for centuries in a landfill or the ocean. Instead, it breaks into smaller and smaller pieces, called microplastics. Burning fossil fuels for energy also releases carbon dioxide, which adds to climate change. Recycling helps, but it does not fully solve the problem. Not every plastic type can be recycled together. Quality gets worse each time plastic is reprocessed. And a large share of plastic waste still does not get recycled at all. That is exactly why using less plastic in the first place, and building real alternatives, like biodegradable plastic, matters just as much as recycling does.

Real-world application: this chain gets worked every day

Petroleum geologists study rock layers to find where source rock actually sits, before anyone drills a single well. Petrochemical engineers run the crackers and reactors that turn crude oil and natural gas into the ethylene and propylene that become real plastic products. Materials scientists test which resin type a product should actually be made from, balancing cost, durability, and how easily it can be recycled afterward. Paleontologists use the same buried-and-preserved idea, on a different material, to read Earth's ancient history from actual fossils. None of this is guesswork, every one of those jobs starts from the same real chain you're about to trace yourself.

History: how we found and used this chain

~300 mya
During the Carboniferous Period, vast swamp forests cover much of the Earth. Dead plant material piles up faster than it can fully decompose in the swamps' low-oxygen water, the start of most of the coal deposits still being mined today.
1859
Edwin Drake drills the first commercial oil well in Titusville, Pennsylvania, launching the modern petroleum industry and the search for buried source rock worldwide.
1907
Leo Baekeland invents Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic, made entirely from fossil-fuel-derived chemicals, not adapted from any natural material like earlier plastics had been.

      📖 Glossary

      Hydrocarbon
      A molecule made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms, the building block of fossil fuels and most plastics.
      Fossil fuel
      An energy resource, oil, natural gas, or coal, formed from the buried remains of ancient living things over millions of years.
      Fossil
      The preserved remains or traces of an ancient organism, bones, shells, or footprints, found in rock.
      Kerogen
      The waxy, carbon-rich material inside buried source rock that heat and pressure slowly convert into oil and natural gas.
      Crude oil
      The raw, unrefined liquid mixture of hydrocarbons pumped from the ground, before it's separated into fuels and other products.
      Polymer
      A long chain molecule made of many smaller repeating units, called monomers, bonded together.
      Monomer
      A single small molecule that bonds with many others like it to form a long chain called a polymer.
      Petrochemical
      A chemical made from crude oil or natural gas, used as a raw ingredient for products like plastic.
      Non-renewable resource
      A resource that forms so slowly, over millions of years, that using it up happens far faster than nature can replace it.
      Recycling
      Reprocessing a used material into new material instead of throwing it away, though not every plastic type can be recycled the same way.
