Three Laws Behind Every Push, Pull, and Launch

A resting object won't move on its own. A moving object won't stop on its own. Force, mass, and acceleration are locked together in one formula. And every push has an equal push back. Those are Newton's three laws, and they explain everything from walking to a rocket launch.

Day 1 · Systems Briefing

Why Things Move the Way They Do

Three laws, discovered by one person, that still describe every motion you'll ever see, from a dropped pencil to a rocket launch. This briefing covers all three before you calculate anything with them yourself.

1Newton's First Law, an object keeps doing what it's already doing

Newton's first law says something that sounds almost too simple: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object already moving keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction, unless a force pushes or pulls on it and changes that. This tendency to keep doing whatever it's already doing is called inertia. A book sitting on a table has inertia. It will sit there forever unless something, a hand, a gust of wind, pushes it. A hockey puck sliding on ice has inertia too, and since ice barely has any friction to slow it down, the puck keeps gliding for a very long time before it finally stops.

Friction is exactly why this law is easy to miss in everyday life. Roll a ball across a rug and it slows down fast, not because it "runs out of motion" on its own, but because friction between the ball and the rug is an unbalanced force constantly working against it. Remove the friction, like a puck on ice or a spacecraft coasting through the emptiness of space, and the object just keeps going, exactly as the first law predicts.

2Newton's Second Law, force, mass, and acceleration

Newton's second law connects three things in one formula: force equals mass times acceleration, written F = m × a. Force is measured in newtons (N). Mass is how much matter an object is made of, measured in kilograms. Acceleration is how quickly speed changes, measured in meters per second squared (m/s²).

Here's what that formula actually predicts. Push a 2 kg cart with 10 newtons of force, and it accelerates at 10 ÷ 2 = 5 m/s². Push a heavier, 5 kg cart with that exact same 10 newtons, and it only accelerates at 10 ÷ 5 = 2 m/s², slower, because more mass resists a change in motion more. That resistance is the same inertia from the first law, just now with a real number attached to it: the more mass something has, the more force it takes to get it accelerating at the same rate.

3Newton's Third Law, every push has a push back

Newton's third law says that forces always come in pairs: for every action, there's an equal and opposite reaction. When you walk, your foot pushes backward against the ground, and the ground pushes forward against your foot, just as hard, that forward push is what actually moves you. Swimming works the same way: you push water backward with your arms, and the water pushes you forward.

A rocket engine is the clearest example of all. It doesn't push against the ground, or the air, or anything outside itself, there's nothing to push against once it's in the vacuum of space. Instead, it burns fuel and throws the exhaust gas backward, out of the engine, at very high speed. By the third law, that backward push on the exhaust creates an equal forward push on the rocket. A released balloon works on the exact same principle: air rushes out the back, and the balloon shoots forward.

🔧 Quick Check

4Put together, all three laws in one system

A real launch uses all three laws at once, not one at a time. Before ignition, the rocket sits perfectly still on the pad, that's the first law, at rest and staying at rest, because gravity pulling down is exactly balanced by the pad pushing up. The instant the engines fire, they throw exhaust gas backward at enormous speed, and the third law creates an equal forward push on the rocket. That forward push is a force, and the second law, F = m × a, is what actually determines how fast the rocket accelerates upward, given its mass. As fuel burns off, the rocket's mass drops, so that same engine force produces more and more acceleration as the flight goes on.

      📖 Glossary

      Inertia
      An object's tendency to keep doing whatever it's already doing, staying at rest or staying in motion, unless a force changes that.
      Force
      A push or a pull on an object, measured in newtons (N).
      Mass
      How much matter an object is made of, measured in kilograms, the same everywhere regardless of gravity.
      Acceleration
      How quickly an object's speed changes, measured in meters per second squared (m/s²).
      Newton's first law
      An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion, unless an unbalanced force acts on it.
      Newton's second law
      Force equals mass times acceleration (F = m × a), more mass needs more force to reach the same acceleration.
      Newton's third law
      For every action, there's an equal and opposite reaction, every push has a push back.
      Friction
      A force that resists motion between two surfaces touching each other, it's what actually slows a rolling ball down.
      Net force
      The overall force left over after every push and pull on an object is combined, this is the number that actually decides acceleration.
      Momentum
      Mass times velocity, conserved when objects push against each other, so a lighter object moves faster to carry the same momentum a heavier one does.
