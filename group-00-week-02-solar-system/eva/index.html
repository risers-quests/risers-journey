The Sun, the Planets, and You

No one can travel to every planet. But we can still learn real, true facts about all of them. We can even build our own model of the whole solar system.

Day 1 · Read and Learn

Meet the Sun and the Eight Planets

Let's learn the real facts: what a star is, how the eight planets are different, what gravity does, and what a day and a year really mean.

Everything in space follows the Sun. Let's meet the Sun first, before we look at any planet. The Sun sits right in the middle. Every planet moves around it.

1The Sun is a star

The Sun is a star. A star is a giant ball of hot gas. It is so hot that it glows. It makes its own light and its own heat.

A planet is different. A planet does not make its own light. It only shines because it bounces light from the Sun back at us.

The Sun sits in the very middle of our solar system. It is huge. More than one million Earths could fit inside it!

The surface of the Sun is about 5,500°C. That is hotter than any oven on Earth. The very center of the Sun is even hotter. It is millions of degrees hot!

The Sun is made mostly of two gases: hydrogen and helium. Light is the fastest thing there is. But the Sun is so far away that its light still takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth. Almost every bit of warmth and light on Earth starts at the Sun.

2The eight planets, in order

Every planet is either rocky or made of gas. Planets close to the Sun are rocky. Planets far from the Sun are gas giants.

🪨 Mercury

The smallest planet. It is closest to the Sun. It is rocky, and covered in craters. It has no moons at all. A Mercury year is only 88 Earth days!

🪨 Venus

Venus is about the same size as Earth. Thick clouds trap in heat. This makes Venus the hottest planet — even hotter than Mercury. Venus also spins the opposite way from most planets.

🪨 Earth

Earth is our home planet. It is the only planet with liquid water on its surface. It is the only one we know that has life. Earth has one moon.

🪨 Mars

Mars is called the "Red Planet." Rusty red dust covers it. Mars has two small moons, named Phobos and Deimos. It also has the tallest volcano in the whole solar system.

☁️ Jupiter

Jupiter is the biggest planet of all. It is a gas giant with a giant storm called the Great Red Spot. The storm is bigger than all of Earth. Jupiter has dozens of moons.

☁️ Saturn

Saturn is famous for its wide, bright rings. The rings are made of countless chunks of ice and rock. Saturn is so light for its size that it could almost float in water.

☁️ Uranus

Uranus is a gas giant tipped almost completely on its side. It rolls along its orbit like a ball, instead of spinning like a top.

☁️ Neptune

Neptune is the windiest planet. It is also the farthest from the Sun. It is a deep blue gas giant with the fastest winds in the whole solar system.

The four planets closest to the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. These are rocky planets. Each one has a hard, solid surface made of rock — the same kind of ground you could stand on.

The four planets farthest from the Sun are Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. These are gas giants. A gas giant is made mostly of gas, all the way through. It has no solid surface at all. Every gas giant is much bigger than any rocky planet.

Between the rocky planets and the gas giants is a wide gap. That gap is called the asteroid belt. More on that soon.

Pluto used to be called the ninth planet. Today, scientists call it a dwarf planet instead. A dwarf planet is round and orbits the Sun, just like a real planet. But it is much smaller. It also shares its part of space with lots of other small icy objects nearby.

3Gravity and orbits

Every planet wants to fly off in a straight line, forever. Something stops it. That something is called gravity. Gravity is an invisible pulling force.

The Sun is so big and so heavy that its gravity pulls on every planet, all the time. That pull curves each planet's path into a loop, instead of a straight line. The curved path a planet takes around the Sun is called its orbit.

If the Sun's gravity ever switched off, every planet would stop curving. It would fly straight off into space.

A year is the time it takes a planet to go all the way around the Sun, one time. Planets close to the Sun have a shorter path and move faster. So their year is short: Mercury's year is only about 88 Earth days. Planets far from the Sun have a longer path and move slower. So their year is long: Neptune's year is about 165 Earth years!

Earth's own year is 365 days. That is exactly why our calendar counts a year as 365 days.

🪐 One steady pull, curving the whole path
Gravity never lets go — that is the whole reason planets stay in their orbits.

4Spinning and days

Every planet also rotates. It spins around an invisible line through its middle, just like a spinning top. That invisible line is called its axis.

A day is the time it takes a planet to spin around one full time. Whichever side of a planet faces the Sun gets daytime. The side facing away gets night.

Earth spins around once in about 24 hours. That is exactly what one Earth day means. It is where the length of every day you've ever lived comes from.

Not every planet spins at the same speed. Jupiter is huge, but it spins very fast. One Jupiter day is only about 10 hours! Venus is the opposite. It spins so slowly that one Venus day is longer than one whole Venus year.

Most planets spin the same direction. But Venus and Uranus spin backwards! Scientists think something huge crashed into them long, long ago. That crash flipped their spin.

🌗 Day side, night side
Only one side of a planet faces the Sun at a time.
The light side faces the Sun — that's daytime.
The dark side faces away — that's nighttime. As the planet spins, every spot takes a turn in both.

5The Moon, asteroids, and comets

The Moon is a big, rocky ball that orbits Earth, the same way Earth orbits the Sun. Earth's own gravity pulls on the Moon and holds it in its orbit.

The Moon does not make its own light. Every bit of its glow in the night sky is really sunlight, bouncing off its rocky surface. The Moon orbits Earth over about a month. As it orbits, we see different amounts of its sunlit side. That changing view is what makes the Moon look like it changes shape, night to night.

Earth is not the only planet with a moon! Mars has two moons. Jupiter and Saturn each have dozens of moons. Mercury and Venus are the only planets with no moon at all.

Not every piece of rock in space became a planet. Between Mars and Jupiter sits the asteroid belt. It is a wide band of countless rocky chunks, called asteroids, all orbiting the Sun together. Scientists think these are leftover pieces. They never joined together to make a real planet.

Farther out, past Neptune, live comets. A comet is a ball of ice, rock, and dust. Most of the time a comet is just a quiet frozen lump. But when it flies close to the Sun, the Sun's heat turns some of its ice into gas. That gas streams off it and makes the comet's long, glowing tail.

History: how we learned all this

1543
Nicolaus Copernicus shows that the Sun, not Earth, sits at the center of the solar system. This was a huge change from what almost everyone believed before.
1609–1619
Johannes Kepler works out the real math of orbits. Planets travel in ovals, not perfect circles. Planets farther from the Sun really do take longer to go around — exactly what you just read above.
1969
NASA's Apollo 11 mission lands the first humans on the Moon. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walk on its surface. They bring real Moon rock back to Earth.

Real-world use: real people use these same facts

Real engineers use these same gravity-and-orbit facts. They launch satellites that circle Earth for GPS and weather forecasts. Each satellite needs exactly the right speed to stay in a stable orbit, instead of falling or flying off. Space agencies also use each planet's real year length to plan launches. They time a launch toward Mars or Jupiter so the planet is actually in the right place when the spacecraft arrives, sometimes years later.
