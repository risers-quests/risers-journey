The Sun, the Planets, and You

Every planet has its own day and night. You are about to learn why, and then prove it for real with your own planet model.

Day 1 · Read and Learn

Meet the Sun and the Eight Planets

Let's learn the real facts: what a star is, how the eight planets are different, what gravity does, and what a day and a year really mean.

Everything in space follows the Sun. Before we look at any planet, let's meet the Sun first. It sits right in the middle. Every planet moves around it.

1The Sun is a star

The Sun is a star. A star is a giant ball of hot gas. It is so hot that it glows. It makes its own light and its own heat.

A planet is different. A planet does not make its own light. It only shines because it reflects light from the Sun.

The Sun sits in the very middle of our solar system. It is huge, more than one million Earths could fit inside it!

The surface of the Sun is about 5,500°C. That is hotter than any oven on Earth. The very center of the Sun is even hotter, millions of degrees!

The Sun is made mostly of two gases: hydrogen and helium. Light is the fastest thing there is, but the Sun is so far away that its light still takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth. Every bit of warmth and almost every bit of light on Earth starts at the Sun.

2The eight planets, in order

Every planet is either rocky or made of gas. Planets close to the Sun are rocky. Planets far from the Sun are gas giants.

🪨 Mercury

Smallest planet, and closest to the Sun. Rocky, and covered in craters. It has no moons at all. A Mercury year is only 88 Earth days!

🪨 Venus

About the same size as Earth. Thick clouds trap heat, making Venus the hottest planet, even hotter than Mercury. Venus also spins the opposite way from most planets.

🪨 Earth

Our home planet. The only planet with liquid water on its surface, and the only one known to have life. Earth has one moon.

🪨 Mars

Called the "Red Planet" because rusty red dust covers it. Mars has two small moons, named Phobos and Deimos, and the tallest volcano in the whole solar system.

☁️ Jupiter

The biggest planet of all, a gas giant with a giant storm called the Great Red Spot, bigger than all of Earth. Jupiter has dozens of moons.

☁️ Saturn

Famous for its wide, bright rings, made of countless chunks of ice and rock. Saturn is so light for its size, it could almost float in water.

☁️ Uranus

A gas giant tipped almost completely on its side, so it rolls along its orbit like a ball instead of spinning like a top.

☁️ Neptune

The windiest planet, and the farthest from the Sun, a deep blue gas giant with the fastest winds in the whole solar system.

The four planets closest to the Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, are rocky planets. Each one has a hard, solid surface made of rock, the same kind of ground you could stand on.

The four planets farthest from the Sun, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, are gas giants. A gas giant is made mostly of gas, all the way through, with no solid surface at all. Every gas giant is much bigger than any rocky planet.

Between the rocky planets and the gas giants is a wide gap. That gap is called the asteroid belt, more on that soon.

Pluto used to be called the ninth planet. Today, scientists call it a dwarf planet instead. A dwarf planet is round and orbits the Sun, just like a real planet, but it is much smaller, and it shares its part of space with lots of other small icy objects nearby.

3Gravity and orbits

Every planet wants to fly off in a straight line, forever. Something stops it. That something is called gravity, an invisible pulling force.

The Sun is so big and so heavy that its gravity pulls on every planet, all the time. That pull curves each planet's path into a loop instead of a straight line. The curved path a planet takes around the Sun is called its orbit.

If the Sun's gravity ever switched off, every planet would stop curving and fly straight off into space.

A year is the time it takes a planet to go all the way around the Sun one time. Planets close to the Sun have a shorter path and move faster, so their year is short: Mercury's year is only about 88 Earth days. Planets far from the Sun have a longer path and move slower, so their year is long: Neptune's year is about 165 Earth years!

Earth's own year is 365 days. That is exactly why our calendar counts a year as 365 days.

🪐 One steady pull, curving the whole path
Gravity never lets go, that is the whole reason planets stay in their orbits.

4Spinning and days

Every planet also rotates, it spins around an invisible line through its middle, called its axis, just like a spinning top.

A day is the time it takes a planet to spin around one full time. Whichever side of a planet faces the Sun gets daytime. The side facing away gets night.

Earth spins around once in about 24 hours, that is exactly what one Earth day means, and where the length of every day you've ever lived comes from.

Not every planet spins at the same speed. Jupiter is huge, but it spins very fast, one Jupiter day is only about 10 hours! Venus is the opposite: it spins so slowly that one Venus day is longer than one whole Venus year.

Most planets spin the same direction. But Venus and Uranus spin backwards! Scientists think something huge crashed into them long, long ago and flipped their spin.

🌗 Day side, night side
Only one side of a planet faces the Sun at a time.
The light side faces the Sun, that's daytime.
The dark side faces away, that's nighttime. As the planet spins, every spot takes a turn in both.

5The Moon, asteroids, and comets

The Moon is a big, rocky ball that orbits Earth, the same way Earth orbits the Sun. Earth's own gravity pulls on the Moon and holds it in its orbit.

The Moon does not make its own light. Every bit of its glow in the night sky is really sunlight, bouncing off its rocky surface. As the Moon orbits Earth over about a month, we see different amounts of its sunlit side, that changing view is what makes the Moon look like it changes shape, night to night.

Earth is not the only planet with a moon! Mars has two moons. Jupiter and Saturn each have dozens of moons. Mercury and Venus are the only planets with no moon at all.

Not every piece of rock in space became a planet. Between Mars and Jupiter sits the asteroid belt, a wide band of countless rocky chunks, called asteroids, all orbiting the Sun together. Scientists think these are leftover pieces that never joined together to make a real planet.

Farther out, past Neptune, live comets, balls of ice, rock, and dust. Most of the time a comet is just a quiet frozen lump. But when it flies close to the Sun, the Sun's heat turns some of its ice into gas, streaming off it and making the comet's long, glowing tail.

History: how we learned all this

1543
Nicolaus Copernicus shows that the Sun, not Earth, sits at the center of the solar system, a huge change from what almost everyone believed before.
1609–1619
Johannes Kepler works out the real math of orbits: planets travel in ovals, not perfect circles, and planets farther from the Sun really do take longer to go around, exactly what you just read above.
1969
NASA's Apollo 11 mission lands the first humans on the Moon. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walk on its surface and bring real Moon rock back to Earth.

Real-world use: real people use these same facts

Real engineers use these same gravity-and-orbit facts to launch satellites that circle Earth for GPS and weather forecasts, each one needs exactly the right speed to stay in a stable orbit instead of falling or flying off. Space agencies also use each planet's real year length to plan when to launch a spacecraft toward Mars or Jupiter, so the planet is actually in the right place by the time the spacecraft arrives, sometimes years later.

    Day 2 · Test Day and Night

    Build a Planet, and Prove Where Day and Night Happen

    Your light stays completely still the whole time, just like the real Sun. Your planet is the one that turns, exactly like a real planet, and that's what actually causes day and night.

    What you're building

    A real physical test of why day and night happen, not a drawing of one. Your light source (a flashlight, lamp, or tea light) is the Sun in this model, and it stays completely still in one spot the entire time. Your planet ball rests in a stand right in front of it, with 4 points marked evenly around its middle, like the 12, 3, 6, and 9 on a clock face. You turn the planet by hand, point by point, and check whether the light is actually hitting each one: lit means Day, dark means Night. That's the whole test, and it matters that only the planet moves: in real life the Sun doesn't circle Earth to make day and night happen, Earth spins while the Sun stays still. If you moved the light around instead of turning your planet, your test would show the wrong thing.
