Every Place on Earth Has Its Own Zoning Code

A real city planner zones neighborhoods by what they're built for. Earth zones whole regions the same way — just by temperature and rain instead of buildings.

Day 1 · Site Survey

What Really Sets a Place's Climate

You can't zone a map without knowing the real rules first. This briefing covers the basics: what sets a place's climate, the five real zones, what else can change the rules, and why it all matters.

Why the same sun treats every place differently

The sun sends out about the same amount of energy toward every part of Earth. But the Sahara Desert gets past 40°C, and Antarctica stays locked in ice. Same sun, very different results. The real reason is geography. A place's climate — the long-term average pattern of temperature and rain in a place, usually measured over many years — comes down to a short list of real rules. That's different from weather, which is just today's conditions outside your window. One hot day doesn't change a place's climate. Years and years of average conditions do. This briefing walks through every rule, one at a time, so your map tomorrow is built on facts, not guesses.

1What actually determines a location's climate

The single biggest rule behind any place's climate is its latitude — how far north or south it sits from the equator, measured in degrees from 0° at the equator to 90° at either pole. Latitude decides one specific thing: the angle sunlight hits the ground at. Near the equator, the sun sits almost straight overhead most of the year, so sunlight lands at a steep angle, close to straight down. That energy lands packed into a small patch of ground — like a spotlight aimed straight at a wall. Near the poles, that same amount of sunlight lands at a shallow, slanted angle instead, so it spreads out over a much bigger patch — like the same spotlight aimed sideways along the wall. It also has to travel through more atmosphere to get there, so some of it gets lost along the way. Spread the same energy over a bigger patch, and every part of that patch gets less of it. That's the real reason the equator runs hot and the poles run cold — not because the poles are farther from the sun in space (a place's distance from the sun barely changes anywhere on Earth), but because of the angle sunlight lands at.

Sunlight hits the equator and the poles at very different angles. Which one gets the more direct hit? Why does a direct hit mean more heat lands there?

Earth's 23.5° tilt adds a seasonal twist — it's why the Northern and Southern Hemispheres take turns leaning toward the sun through the year, giving most places a warmer season and a cooler one. But the main pattern never flips: averaged across a whole year, the equator is still Earth's hottest zone, and it still gets colder the farther you travel toward either pole. That latitude rule is the baseline every climate-zone map is built on.

2The five real climate zoning districts

Scientists sort the whole planet into a small set of real, defined climate zones — regions that share a similar temperature pattern and rain pattern, based on years of real recorded data. The most-used system is the Köppen climate classification, and a simplified version of it zones almost the entire planet into five major districts.

The tropical zone sits close to the equator, roughly between 23.5°N and 23.5°S. It stays warm to hot all year, with almost no seasonal temperature swing — instead of a hot season and a cold season, tropical places usually swing between a wet season and a dry season. The arid (dry) zone is zoned by something completely different: not temperature, but rain. A place gets zoned arid when it loses more water to evaporation than it gets back as rainfall — which means an arid zone can be scorching hot (the Sahara) or genuinely cold (parts of the Gobi and the high steppe), as long as it stays dry. The temperate zone covers the mid-latitudes, roughly 23.5° to 40°, and runs through four real, distinct seasons with a moderate temperature range overall. The continental zone sits farther out still, deep in the interior of large landmasses, and swings hard between hot summers and brutally cold winters. The polar zone covers the highest latitudes, generally past the Arctic and Antarctic circles near 66.5° — it's the coldest district on the map, and even its warmest month stays under about 10°C, with very little rain.

3What else can change a district's rules

Latitude sets the baseline zoning, but it isn't the only rule a real city planner has to check — three more factors can shift a place's actual climate away from what its latitude alone would set.

Elevation is the first: temperature drops about 6.5°C for every 1,000 meters you climb, because air thins out and holds less heat at higher altitude. That's why Mount Kilimanjaro carries snow at its summit even though it sits almost exactly on the equator — elevation overrides the latitude rule entirely at that height.

Ocean currents are the second: these are big, moving rivers of ocean water that carry real heat around the planet. A warm current like the Gulf Stream drags heat from the tropics up toward Western Europe's coastline, keeping it noticeably milder than other places at the same latitude; a cold current does the opposite, cooling — and often drying out — the coastline it passes.

Distance from the ocean is the third rule, and it's related but different: water heats up and cools down much more slowly than land does. A coastline stays cushioned by that slow-changing water nearby, so its temperature swings stay moderate. Move deep into the interior of a continent, far from any ocean's cushioning, and that protection disappears — summers run hotter and winters run colder than a coastline sees at the very same latitude. That's part of why the continental zone gets its own zoning code at all.

Putting it together: how the zoning map shapes life on Earth

Every climate zone's real temperature-and-rain pattern decides which biomes — large communities of plants and animals built for a region's climate — can exist there. A tropical zone's warmth and rain support a tropical rainforest biome, packed with more species than almost anywhere on Earth. A polar zone's cold and near-zero rain support tundra, where only a thin layer of soil ever thaws and only low, tough plants survive. A continental zone's harsh temperature swings support boreal forest (taiga), built from trees tough enough to survive both extremes. None of that is random — it's the zoning code written directly into the living world.

That same zoning code shapes human life just as directly. Farming depends on matching crops to a region's real climate zone — rice needs the warmth and standing water of a wet tropical or subtropical climate, while wheat does well in a temperate zone's moderate rain and four real seasons. Where people build cities, how homes get designed, and even old trade routes have all followed this same climate zoning for thousands of years. Zoning a place's climate isn't just an exercise — it's the same rule people have always used to decide where and how to build.

History: how we learned to map the world's climate zones

1817
Alexander von Humboldt publishes the first isotherm map — lines connecting spots with the same average temperature across the globe. It was the first real proof that latitude alone doesn't decide climate, which sent scientists looking for other rules, like ocean currents.
1884
Wladimir Köppen publishes an early sketch connecting temperature zones to the plant boundaries being mapped at the time — the first real link between climate data and the biomes it produces.
1900
Köppen publishes the first full version of his climate classification system, zoning the world into lettered districts based on real temperature and rain data. He updates it again in 1918.
1936
Köppen, working with Rudolf Geiger, publishes the final, fully mapped version — the direct ancestor of the Köppen-Geiger climate map scientists still use today.

Real-world application: the zoning map behind every building code

Building codes use real climate-zone maps — built on exactly this evidence — to set the rules for a home's insulation, heating, and cooling, because a house built for a polar or continental district needs totally different design than one built for a tropical district. Farmers use the same kind of map in plant hardiness zones, based on a region's typical lowest winter temperature, to know which crops will actually survive there. It's the same zoning code this whole reading is built on, just applied to a building permit or a seed packet instead of a map.

    Don't say "I'm going to talk about climate zones." Instead, hold up your strip map and ask the room: "If you had to move to one end of this strip, which end would you pick — and why?" Let a few people answer before you explain the real rules.

      📖 Glossary

      Climate
      The long-term average pattern of temperature and rain in a place, usually measured over many years.
      Weather
      The day-to-day conditions in a place — today's temperature, rain, or wind — different from climate.
      Latitude
      A place's distance north or south of the equator, measured in degrees from 0° to 90°.
      Equator
      The imaginary line around Earth at 0° latitude, halfway between the North and South Poles.
      Sun angle
      The angle sunlight hits Earth's surface at — steep near the equator, shallow near the poles.
      Tropical zone
      The climate zone near the equator; warm to hot all year, often with heavy rain.
      Arid zone
      A dry climate zone defined by very low rainfall, not by temperature.
      Temperate zone
      A mid-latitude climate zone with four distinct seasons and a moderate temperature range.
      Continental zone
      A mid-to-high-latitude climate zone far from the ocean, with big swings between hot summers and cold winters.
      Polar zone
      The coldest climate zone, near the poles, where even the warmest month stays cold.
      Elevation
      Height above sea level; temperature drops as elevation increases.
      Ocean current
      A large moving river of ocean water that carries heat toward or away from nearby coastlines.
      Biome
      A large community of plants and animals built for a region's climate.
