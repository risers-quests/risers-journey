Day 1 · Case Briefing

What Actually Controls a Location's Climate

You can't identify a climate zone without knowing what actually builds one. This briefing covers the full case: the one factor that sets the baseline, the five real zones, what else can shift the evidence, and why it all matters.

Why the same sun treats every location differently

The sun sends out roughly the same amount of energy toward every part of Earth. Yet the Sahara Desert regularly bakes past 40°C while Antarctica sits locked in ice, and both get hit by the very same star. The reason isn't the sun — it's geography. Every location's real climate — the long-term average pattern of temperature and precipitation a place experiences, usually measured over 30 years or more — comes down to a short list of real, checkable factors. That's different from weather, the day-to-day conditions outside your window today; one unusually hot or rainy day doesn't change a location's climate, but decades of average conditions do. This case file walks through every real factor, in order, so nothing you conclude tomorrow is a guess.

1What actually determines a location's climate

The single biggest factor behind any location's climate is its latitude — how far north or south it sits from the equator, measured in degrees from 0° at the equator itself to 90° at either pole. Latitude controls something very specific: the angle at which sunlight actually strikes the ground. Near the equator, the sun sits close to directly overhead for most of the year, so sunlight arrives at a steep angle, close to straight down — that energy lands concentrated on a small patch of surface. Near the poles, that exact same amount of sunlight arrives at a shallow, glancing angle instead, so it spreads out over a much larger patch of surface, and it also has to travel through more atmosphere to get there, losing extra energy along the way. Spread the same energy over a bigger patch, and every square meter of that patch gets less of it. That's the entire reason the equator runs hot and the poles run cold — not distance from the sun in space (Earth's distance from the sun barely changes from one location to another), but the angle sunlight lands at.

🔬 Angle Check

Earth's 23.5° tilt on its axis adds a seasonal wrinkle — it's why the Northern and Southern Hemispheres take turns leaning toward the sun through the year, giving most locations a warmer season and a cooler one. But the underlying pattern never flips: averaged across a full year, the equator is still Earth's hottest zone, and temperatures still trend colder the farther you travel toward either pole. That latitude-driven gradient is the baseline every climate zone system on file is built on.

2The five real climate zones — reading the case file

Climatologists sort the whole planet into a small set of real, defined climate zones — regions that share a similar temperature pattern and precipitation pattern, based on decades of real recorded data. The most widely used system is the Köppen climate classification, and a simplified version of it sorts almost everywhere on Earth into five major zones.

The tropical zone sits close to the equator, roughly between 23.5°N and 23.5°S. It stays warm to hot all year, with little seasonal temperature swing — instead of a hot season and a cold season, tropical locations usually swing between a wet season and a dry season. The arid (dry) zone is defined by something completely different: not temperature, but precipitation. A location counts as arid when it loses more water to evaporation than it receives as rainfall — which means an arid zone can be scorching (the Sahara) or genuinely cold (parts of the Gobi and the high steppe), as long as it stays dry. The temperate zone covers the mid-latitudes, roughly 23.5° to 40°, and runs through four real, distinct seasons with a moderate overall temperature range. The continental zone sits farther still from the equator, deep in the interior of large landmasses, and swings hard between hot summers and brutally cold winters. The polar zone covers the highest latitudes, generally above the Arctic and Antarctic circles near 66.5° — it's the coldest zone on the case file, and even its warmest month averages below about 10°C, with very little precipitation.

3What else can shift the evidence

Latitude sets the baseline, but it isn't the only variable a real climate detective has to check — three more factors can shift a location's actual climate away from what its latitude alone would predict.

Elevation is the first: temperature drops roughly 6.5°C for every 1,000 meters you climb, because air thins and holds less heat at altitude. That's why Mount Kilimanjaro carries snow at its summit despite sitting almost exactly on the equator — elevation overrides the latitude evidence entirely at that height.

Ocean currents are the second: these are large-scale movements of ocean water that carry real heat energy around the planet. A warm current like the Gulf Stream drags heat from the tropics up toward Western Europe's coastline, keeping it noticeably milder than other locations at the same latitude; a cold current does the opposite, chilling — and often drying out — the coastline it passes.

Distance from the ocean is the third, and it's related but different: water heats up and cools down far more slowly than land does. A coastline stays cushioned by that slow-changing water nearby, so its temperature swings stay moderate. Push deep into the interior of a continent, far from any ocean's moderating reach, and that cushion disappears — summers run hotter and winters run colder than a coastline sees at the very same latitude. That's a real part of why the continental zone exists as its own case file entirely.

Putting it together: how climate zones shape life on Earth

Every climate zone's real temperature-and-precipitation pattern determines which biomes — large communities of plants and animals adapted to a region's climate — can exist there. A tropical zone's warmth and rainfall support a tropical rainforest biome, packed with more species than almost anywhere else on Earth. A polar zone's cold and near-absent precipitation support tundra, where only a thin layer of soil ever thaws and only low, hardy plants survive. A continental zone's harsh temperature swings support boreal forest (taiga), built from trees tough enough to survive both extremes. None of that is an accident — it's climate evidence written directly into the living world.

That same evidence shapes human life just as directly. Agriculture depends on matching crops to a region's real climate zone — rice needs the warmth and standing water of a wet tropical or subtropical climate, while wheat thrives in a temperate zone's moderate rainfall and four real seasons. Where people build cities, how homes get designed and insulated, and even historic trade routes have all followed the same climate-zone evidence for thousands of years. Identifying a location's climate zone isn't just a classification exercise — it's the same evidence people have always used to decide where and how to live.

History: how we learned to map the world's climate evidence

1817
Alexander von Humboldt publishes the first isotherm map — lines connecting points of equal temperature across the globe. It's the first real evidence that latitude alone doesn't decide climate, which sent scientists looking for the other factors, like ocean currents.
1884
Wladimir Köppen publishes an early sketch connecting temperature zones to the vegetation boundaries being mapped at the time — the first real link between climate data and the biomes it produces.
1900
Köppen publishes the first full version of his climate classification system, sorting the world into lettered zones based on real temperature and precipitation data. He revises it further in 1918.
1936
Köppen, working with Rudolf Geiger, publishes the refined, fully mapped version of the system — the direct ancestor of the Köppen-Geiger climate map still used by scientists today.

Real-world application: climate zones decide how buildings and crops get designed

Building codes use real climate-zone maps — built on exactly this evidence — to set requirements for a home's insulation, heating, and cooling, because a house built for a polar or continental zone needs completely different design than one built for a tropical zone. Farmers use the same kind of evidence in plant hardiness zone maps, based on a region's typical minimum winter temperature, to know which crops and plants will actually survive there. It's the same case file this whole reading is built on, just applied to a construction permit or a seed packet instead of a mystery location.

    Day 2 · Build the Rig

    Build the Evidence Rig — Then Identify the Mystery Locations

    Your case this week: build a real physical instrument that proves sun angle changes heating, then use your climate-zone reference chart to identify a set of mystery locations from proton-count-style evidence — their real temperature and precipitation data.

    What you're building

    A real, physical sun-angle evidence rig — not a drawing or a diagram of one. Mount your light source on a fixed stand at a set height above your recording surface. Using your angle guide, shine the light straight down (close to 90°, standing in for the equator) onto the paper and trace the lit patch; then reposition the light to a shallow angle (around 30°, standing in for a location near a pole) and trace that patch too. Every material you use has to represent one real, correctly-set part of the rig — a flashlight propped up at a random angle "because it looks right" doesn't count as evidence.

    Finished, it should look like this: a fixed light stand over a sheet of grid paper, with two traced-and-labeled patches on it — one small, bright circle labeled "90° — Equator" and one larger, dimmer oval labeled "30° — Near-pole" — with each patch's measured area written right next to it.

    📎 Reference: expected result
    Small, bright patch (steep angle) Large, dim patch (shallow angle)

    This is the pattern you're proving: same light, same amount of time, two very different patch sizes. The bigger patch means the same energy is more spread out — dimmer per square inch.

      📖 Glossary

      Climate
      The long-term average pattern of temperature and precipitation in a place, usually measured over many years.
      Weather
      The day-to-day conditions in a place — today's temperature, rain, or wind — different from climate.
      Latitude
      A location's distance north or south of the equator, measured in degrees from 0° to 90°.
      Equator
      The imaginary line around Earth at 0° latitude, halfway between the North and South Poles.
      Sun angle
      The angle at which sunlight strikes Earth's surface — steep near the equator, shallow near the poles.
      Tropical zone
      The climate zone near the equator; warm to hot all year, often with heavy rainfall.
      Arid zone
      A dry climate zone defined by very low precipitation, not by temperature.
      Temperate zone
      A mid-latitude climate zone with four distinct seasons and a moderate temperature range.
      Continental zone
      A mid-to-high-latitude climate zone far from the ocean, with big swings between hot summers and cold winters.
      Polar zone
      The coldest climate zone, near the poles, where even the warmest month stays cold.
      Elevation
      Height above sea level; temperature drops as elevation increases.
      Ocean current
      A large-scale movement of ocean water that carries heat toward or away from nearby coastlines.
      Biome
      A large community of plants and animals adapted to a region's climate.
