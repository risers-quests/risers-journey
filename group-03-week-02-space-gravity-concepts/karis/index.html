Part 1 · Mass, Weight & Orbit

Before You Fly, Know the Physics

Two ideas mission control can't get wrong: what actually changes when a ship leaves Earth, and why an orbiting object never just falls out of the sky.

1Mass and weight aren't the same thing

Mass is how much matter something is made of. It's the same everywhere in the universe — on Earth, on the Moon, floating in empty space. A 120 kg equipment crate has a mass of 120 kg no matter where mission control sends it.

Weight is different. Weight is how hard gravity pulls on that mass. Gravity isn't the same strength everywhere. On Earth, gravity is strong enough that the crate weighs 1,200 newtons. The Moon's gravity is only about 1/6th as strong as Earth's. So the exact same crate, with the exact same mass, weighs far less there. Nothing about the crate itself changed. Only how hard gravity was pulling on it changed.

This is why astronauts can carry equipment on the Moon that would be too heavy to lift on Earth. Their actual mass — and the effort it takes to speed them up or stop them — stays exactly the same.

2Why a satellite doesn't just fall

Gravity never stops pulling a satellite toward the planet it orbits. Not for one second. So why doesn't it just fall straight down?

The answer is that an orbiting object actually is constantly falling. It's moving forward so fast, sideways, that by the time gravity has pulled it down a little, the curve of the planet has dropped away beneath it by about the same amount. It keeps falling, and it keeps missing. That endless falling-and-missing is exactly what an orbit is.

Slow the satellite down too much, and it falls in for real. Speed it up too much, and it flies off into space instead. Get the sideways speed exactly right, and it just keeps missing the planet, forever. A satellite orbiting close to Earth, for example, completes one full orbit in about 2 hours — low, fast, and constantly missing.

Part 2 · Breaking Free, Falling Back

Leaving Orbit, and Coming Back Down

Two more real numbers mission control lives and dies by: how fast you need to go to actually leave a planet, and how fast something speeds up once gravity is the only thing acting on it.

3Escape velocity

Escape velocity is the minimum speed something needs to break completely free of a planet's gravity, so it never falls back. It isn't the same number everywhere. It depends on how strong that planet's gravity actually is, which depends on the planet's mass.

Bigger, heavier planets have stronger gravity, so it takes more speed to break free of them. Earth's escape velocity is about 11 km/s. The Moon's is only about 2.4 km/s, because the Moon has far less mass and a much weaker pull. This is exactly why it was so much easier for the Apollo missions to launch back off the Moon than it was to launch off Earth in the first place.

4Free fall under real gravity

An object in free fall is falling with nothing but gravity acting on it — no engine, no air resistance counted. It does not fall at a constant speed. It speeds up the whole way down, at a fixed rate.

On the Moon, that rate is about 1.6 m/s². Its speed goes up by 1.6 meters per second for every second it falls. That's much gentler than Earth's own 9.8 m/s². This is exactly why an astronaut in a heavy suit can still fall and land safely on the Moon in situations that would be dangerous on Earth. Real mission planners use this exact number to predict how hard a lander will touch down.

History: how we learned to navigate space

1638
Galileo Galilei shows that all falling objects speed up at the same rate, no matter their mass. A heavy object and a light one, dropped together, hit the ground at the same instant. This is exactly why "mass" and "weight" had to become two separate ideas.
1957
The Soviet Union launches Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite. It proved for the first time that an object really could be given enough sideways speed to stay in a stable orbit instead of falling back to Earth.
1969
NASA's Apollo 11 lands the first humans on the Moon. The lunar module's landing, and its later launch back off the Moon, both used the exact same free-fall and escape-velocity numbers you just read about — just for real, with astronauts' lives depending on getting them right.

    Part 4 · Build Day

    Build a Tabletop Gravity Well

    Every calculation you fired in Part 3 depended on one idea: mass bends the space around it, and that bend is what we call gravity. Today you build a model you can actually see it in.

    What you're building

    A tabletop gravity well. Fabric stretched drum-tight across a frame stands in for space itself. A heavy ball set in the middle sags the fabric into a dip — that dip is the "gravity well" a real star or planet creates in the space around it. A planet doesn't reach out and grab a spacecraft; it bends the space near it, and anything moving through that bent space curves. Roll a marble across the stretched fabric and its path bends toward the dip the same way — sometimes enough to fall in, sometimes just enough to loop around forever. That looping-around-forever case is exactly what an orbit is, and it's what you'll be trying to produce by hand.
