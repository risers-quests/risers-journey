Day 1 · Systems Briefing

How Rock Becomes the Ground You Stand On

Loose dirt looks the same everywhere, but it isn't. It's built from four real ingredients, broken down and layered over centuries. This briefing covers exactly how that structure forms, before you build a real layered profile of your own.

Why "soil" isn't just one thing

Say the word "soil" and it's easy to picture one uniform brown substance. It isn't. Soil is a mix of four separate ingredients. They came from two very different processes: weathered rock provides the minerals, and living things provide the organic matter. The two combine together over an amount of time you can't rush. Before you can build a model that's actually accurate, you need to know exactly what's really in soil and how it actually got there.

1The four ingredients — and how they got there

Soil is a mix of four real ingredients: mineral particles (tiny broken-down pieces of rock), organic matter (decayed plant and animal material, called humus once it's fully broken down), water, and air. The water and air fill the small spaces between the solid particles.

None of those minerals started out as soil-sized grains. They all came from weathering, the slow breakdown of solid rock into smaller and smaller particles. Physical weathering breaks rock apart without changing what it's made of. Water seeps into a crack, freezes, and expands. That widens the crack a little more every freeze-thaw cycle, until the rock finally splits. Wind, water, and moving ice also wear rock surfaces down, the same way sandpaper wears down wood.

Chemical weathering works differently. Rain is slightly acidic. Over long stretches of time, it slowly dissolves and changes the minerals inside a rock, weakening it until it crumbles more easily. Most real rock breaks down from both processes working together. That broken-down mineral material — not a soil bag from a store — forms the raw base every soil profile is built on.

2Horizons: the layers you're about to measure

A vertical slice straight down through real ground is called a soil profile. It's made of distinct horizontal layers called horizons.

At the very top, the O horizon is a thin layer of mostly organic material — fallen leaves and other plant matter in different stages of decay — sitting right on the surface. Below it, the A horizon (what most people just call topsoil) is dark, crumbly, mineral soil mixed with organic matter. It's packed with plant roots and living organisms, and it's the layer almost all plant growth actually depends on.

Below that, the B horizon (subsoil) is lighter in color and has far less organic matter. It's mostly where minerals washed down from the layers above end up collecting. Roots can reach it, but find fewer nutrients there. At the bottom, the C horizon is partly weathered parent material — rock that's started breaking down but hasn't gone nearly as far as true soil. It sits right on top of solid, unweathered bedrock.

Not every location has all of these fully developed. But the O and A horizons together are what most people mean when they say "topsoil," and they're almost always the shallowest, most fragile layer of the whole profile.

3Soil texture: sand, silt, and clay

Beyond its layers, soil also has a texture — the mix of different-sized mineral particles it's made of. Sand particles are the largest of the three and feel gritty. Silt particles are medium-sized and feel smooth, almost like flour. Clay particles are the smallest by far, and feel sticky and dense when wet.

Particle size controls how soil handles water. Sand's large particles leave large gaps between them, so water drains through quickly but doesn't stick around long enough to be held. Clay's tiny particles pack together tightly with almost no gap at all, so water drains through very slowly, but a lot of it gets held once it's in. Silt sits in between both.

A soil that's a balanced mix of all three — not dominated by any one of them — is called loam. It's considered the best all-purpose texture for growing most plants, because it drains reasonably well without draining away the water and nutrients a plant actually needs. Too much sand, and nutrients wash straight through before roots can use them. Too much clay, and water can pool at the surface, or roots can't get enough air.

4Put together, it's a structure you can measure

A full soil profile only exists because two separate processes run at once. Weathering supplies the raw mineral material — sand, silt, and clay-sized rock particles — from the bottom up. Decomposers finish the other half: worms, insects, fungi, and bacteria break down dead leaves, roots, and other organic material into humus, the dark, nutrient-rich matter that gives topsoil its color and its fertility.

Earthworms do double duty while they're at it. As they tunnel through soil, they physically mix and loosen it, opening up more of the air spaces soil needs as its fourth ingredient. None of this happens quickly. Because weathering and decomposition both run slowly, it can take several hundred to well over a thousand years for natural processes to build just one inch (about 2.5 cm) of real topsoil. That's exactly why a layered profile is worth measuring carefully rather than guessing at — every centimeter you record represents a stretch of time no single build session comes close to.

History: how we learned to study the ground

1870s
Vasily Dokuchaev, a Russian geologist considered the founder of modern soil science, shows that soil is a distinct natural body formed by the interaction of climate, living organisms, parent rock, and time — not just "broken-up rock."
1930s
The Dust Bowl: years of drought plus soil-damaging farming practices strip away protective topsoil across the U.S. Great Plains, and windstorms carry millions of tons of it away.
1935
In response, the U.S. creates the Soil Conservation Service, led by Hugh Hammond Bennett — the first national effort to actively study and fight soil erosion.
🌪️ Learning From the Dust Bowl

In the 1930s, years of drought plus soil-damaging farming stripped away huge amounts of topsoil across the U.S. Great Plains, and windstorms carried it away. Why did losing that established topsoil hurt so much more than losing the same depth of subsoil or parent material would have?

Real-world application: this structure gets engineered every day

Farmers test a field's exact soil texture and its horizon depth before planting. That lets them choose crops that actually match what's really there instead of guessing. Civil engineers test soil composition and drainage before pouring a foundation, since clay-heavy ground can shift or hold water in ways that crack a structure years later, while sandy ground can settle unevenly. Conservationists measure topsoil depth over time to track whether erosion-control methods like terracing and cover crops are actually working — the same way you're about to measure your own model's layers instead of eyeballing them.

    Day 2 · Build the Profile

    Build a Real Soil Profile — Then Measure It

    Build one real layered soil-profile model, then measure it with a ruler. This isn't decoration — your model has to have real, distinct layers with real numbers behind them.

    What you're building

    A real, physical 3-layer soil profile model inside a clear container — not a drawing of one. At the bottom goes a layer of gravel or rock chips, standing in for bedrock and the barely-weathered C horizon. On top of that goes a layer of plain soil, standing in for the mineral-heavy subsoil (B horizon). On top of that goes a layer of soil mixed with visible organic material — crushed leaves, grass clippings, or compost — standing in for the topsoil (O and A horizons together). Every layer in your model needs a real, labeled match to a real horizon, not just a name copied from the reading — an engineer who can't say why gravel stands in for bedrock hasn't actually made the connection yet. And a claim about a layer's thickness only counts once you've measured it.

    Finished, it should look like a clear jar with three visibly different bands stacked inside it: a pale gravel layer at the bottom, a plain soil layer in the middle, and a darker, leaf-flecked soil layer on top — every layer labeled on the outside of the jar, and every layer's thickness marked in centimeters, not guessed by eye.
