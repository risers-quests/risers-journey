Mission Meridian-2: Earth to Mars Transfer

Meridian-2 is a probe, not a crewed ship — no life-support margin to fall back on. Part 1 · The Pull That Never Lets Go

Gravity and the Inverse-Square Law

Before you can fly a real transfer orbit, you need the one force that shapes every leg of it: gravity. Not "things fall down" — the actual relationship mission control uses to predict exactly how hard gravity pulls, at any distance.

1What gravity actually is

Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass. That's not just a saying — it's a real rule called Newton's law of universal gravitation. You feel Earth's pull because Earth is huge and close to you. You don't feel the Moon's pull, even though the Moon has plenty of mass, because it's very far away. You don't feel any pull from the person standing next to you, because a person's mass is too small to notice. So what decides how strong a pull is? Just two things: how much mass both objects have, and how far apart they are. More mass makes a stronger pull. More distance makes a weaker pull. That's the whole idea — and it's the same rule real mission control teams use to plan a spacecraft's path to another planet.

2Why distance matters so much

Here's the tricky part: gravity's pull doesn't just get weaker with distance. It gets weaker by the square of the distance. This is called the inverse-square law. If you move twice as far from a planet, the pull isn't cut in half — it's cut to a quarter (because 2 × 2 = 4, so the pull becomes 1/4 as strong). If you move three times as far, the pull doesn't drop to a third — it drops to a ninth (because 3 × 3 = 9). This is why a spacecraft moving away from a planet can lose most of its pull faster than you might expect. Forgetting to square the distance is the most common mistake people make with this kind of problem.

Part 2 · Thrust and the Orbit It Buys You

Force, Acceleration & Orbital Velocity

Gravity explains the pull. This part covers the other half: how a spacecraft's own engines fight back against it, and what it actually takes to stay in a stable orbit instead of falling in or flying off.

3Newton's second law: force from acceleration

Every real thruster burn comes down to one simple rule: force = mass × acceleration (F = ma). Acceleration just means how fast the velocity is changing — you find it by dividing the change in velocity (called Δv) by the time it takes. A real engineer sizing a rocket burn always does the same two steps. First, find the acceleration needed (Δv ÷ time). Then multiply by the spacecraft's mass to get the force the thrusters need to produce. A heavier spacecraft needs more force to reach the same acceleration as a lighter one. The rule doesn't change — it just demands more force when there's more mass.

⚙️ Quick Check

Two identical thrusters each need to produce the same acceleration, but Probe A is twice as heavy as Probe B. Which thruster has to produce more force, and why, based on Newton's second law?

4Orbital velocity: falling and missing

An orbit is one of the strangest ideas in this whole rotation. An orbiting object is actually falling toward whatever it orbits, the whole time — gravity never turns off. So what keeps it from crashing? Its own sideways speed. It moves forward so fast that by the time gravity pulls it down a little, the ground has curved away underneath it by about the same amount. So it keeps falling, and it keeps missing, forever, tracing out a loop. That sideways speed is called orbital velocity. It works just like any speed — distance ÷ time — but on a circular path instead of a straight line: the distance all the way around one orbit, divided by the time it takes to complete one lap. A tight, fast orbit and a wide, slow orbit can both work. What matters is that the sideways speed exactly balances how hard gravity pulls inward at that distance.

Part 3 · Breaking Free, Falling Back

Escape Velocity & Free Fall

One last pair of ideas before you fly the actual mission: what it takes to leave a planet's gravity behind for good, and what happens to something that doesn't — it just falls.

5Escape velocity

Escape velocity is the slowest speed an object can go to break completely free of a planet's gravity — for good, with no more engine burns needed. A planet with more mass holds onto things with a stronger pull, so it takes a higher speed to escape it. That's why leaving a small body like the Moon takes much less speed than leaving Earth. And leaving a giant planet like Jupiter takes much more speed than leaving Earth. Escape velocity is not the same as orbital velocity. Orbital velocity keeps you circling a planet forever. Escape velocity is what it takes to leave and never come back.

6Free fall under real gravity

An object in free fall is falling with nothing but gravity acting on it — no air resistance counted. It does not fall at a constant speed. It speeds up the whole way down, at a fixed rate. On Earth, that rate is g = 9.8 m/s². That means its speed goes up by 9.8 meters per second for every second it falls. After 1 second, it's moving 9.8 m/s. After 2 seconds, 19.6 m/s. After 4 seconds, almost 40 m/s. That steady speeding-up, not a constant speed, is why a fall that starts slow ends fast. Real engineers use this exact number to predict how hard something will hit the ground.

History: how we learned to fly this way

1609–1619
Johannes Kepler works out the real shape of orbits from decades of careful observations: planets travel in ovals, not perfect circles, and sweep out equal areas in equal times. His laws describe exactly how orbits behave — but not yet why.
1687
Isaac Newton publishes the law of universal gravitation, finally explaining WHY Kepler's orbits work the way they do: one inverse-square force, acting between any two masses, is enough to produce every orbit in the solar system.
1925
Walter Hohmann works out the most fuel-efficient way to move a spacecraft from one circular orbit to another — a long, looping ellipse that just touches both orbits. Real missions to Mars still fly this same "Hohmann transfer" today, including Meridian-2's own route.

    Part 5 · Build Day

    Build a Tabletop Gravity Well

    Every calculation you fired in Part 3 depended on one idea: mass bends the space around it, and that bend is what we call gravity. Today you build a model where you can actually see it happen.

    What you're building

    A tabletop gravity well. Fabric stretched tight across a frame stands in for space itself. A heavy ball set in the middle sags the fabric into a dip, that dip is the "gravity well" a real star or planet creates in the space around it. A planet doesn't reach out and grab a spacecraft. It bends the space near it, and anything moving through that bent space curves along with it. Roll a marble across the stretched fabric and its path bends toward the dip the same way, sometimes enough to fall in, sometimes just enough to loop around forever. That looping-around-forever case is exactly what an orbit is, and it's what you'll try to produce by hand.

      📖 Glossary

      Gravity
      An invisible pulling force between any two objects with mass — stronger for bigger masses, weaker with more distance.
      Inverse-square law
      Doubling the distance between two objects cuts gravity's pull to 1/4, not 1/2 — the pull always shrinks with the SQUARE of the distance.
      Newton's second law
      Force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma) — a heavier object needs more force to speed up at the same rate.
      Orbital velocity
      The exact sideways speed that lets an object keep "falling" toward a planet while its curve keeps carrying it past — a stable orbit.
      Escape velocity
      The minimum speed something needs to break completely free of a planet's gravity, so it never falls back.
      Free fall
      Falling with nothing but gravity acting on you — no air resistance, no engine. Speed increases steadily the whole way down.
      g (acceleration due to gravity)
      How much a falling object's speed increases every second — 9.8 m/s² on Earth.
      Momentum
      Mass times velocity — conserved in a collision, so a light object flies off faster when it takes momentum from a heavier one.
      Hohmann transfer
      The most fuel-efficient path between two circular orbits — a long, looping ellipse that just touches both. Real Mars missions still use it.
